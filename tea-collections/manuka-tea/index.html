Therapeutic Benefits of Sweet Tea

Sweet tea (Lithocarpus polystachyus Rehd.) has been consumed as herbal tea to prevent and manage diabetes for a long time. Recent studies indicate that sweet tea is rich in a variety of bioactive compounds, especially a class of nonclassical flavonoids, dihydrochalcones. In order to provide a better understanding of sweet tea and its main dihydrochalcones on human health, this review mainly summarizes related literature in the recent ten years, with the potential molecular mechanisms emphatically discussed. Phlorizin, phloretin, and trilobatin, three natural sweeteners, are the main dihydrochalcones in sweet tea. In addition, sweet tea and its dihydrochalcones exhibit plenty of health benefits, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, cardioprotective, hepatoprotective, antidiabetic, and anticancer effects, which are associated with the regulation of different molecular targets and signaling pathways. Therefore, sweet tea, as a rare natural source of dihydrochalcones, can be processed and developed into nutraceuticals or functional foods, with the potential application in the prevention and management of certain chronic diseases.

The Harvest

Whispers of the Forest: The Artisan Harvest of New Zealand Manuka

Deep in the heart of Aotearoa's untamed wilderness, far beyond the reach of paved roads and the clamor of modern life, lies a world ruled by ancient rhythms. Here, the morning mist clings stubbornly to the emerald canopy of the old-growth forest, and the air is thick with the scent of damp earth and moss. This is the domain of the New Zealand smallholder—not a farmer of vast, flat pastures, but a steward of the rugged, primeval hills.

As dawn breaks, the silence of the deep woods is broken only by the melodic chimes of the native bellbird. A lone farmer makes his way up the steep, winding trails, his boots sinking slightly into the soft, leaf-strewn forest floor. He is surrounded by towering silver ferns and ancient Rimu trees, but his destination is a dense grove further up the ridge: the wild Manuka thickets.

Manuka is a resilient native shrub, famous worldwide for its healing properties, but out here in the wild, it is simply a part of the land's soul. During the short blooming window, the bushes are dusted with delicate white and pale-pink flowers, releasing a sharp, earthy, and subtly sweet fragrance into the crisp mountain air.

For this small farmer, harvesting Manuka is not a job of heavy machinery or mass production. It is an art form defined by patience and respect. Standing before the tangled branches, he begins the meticulous process of hand-picking. With weathered hands that know the texture of every leaf, he carefully selects only the finest young tips, blossoms, and leaves.

Every movement is deliberate. Artificial machines would strip the branches bare, damaging the plant and disrupting the fragile ecosystem. Instead, manual foraging ensures that the shrub is gently pruned, allowing it to thrive and bloom again the following year. He leaves plenty behind for the wild bees that hum quietly in the background, knowing that they, too, rely on this precious nectar.

As the canvas sack over his shoulder slowly fills, the sun climbs higher, piercing through the dense forest canopy to cast golden beams on the forest floor. The work is physically demanding—his back aches, and his fingers are stained with the plant's natural oils—yet there is a profound sense of peace.

By the time he makes his descent back to his small, off-grid homestead, the shadows have begun to lengthen. The harvested Manuka, gathered with such quiet dedication, will soon be naturally dried for artisanal teas or slowly distilled into pure, potent essential oils.

In a world that constantly demands faster, cheaper, and more, this small farmer in the deep woods of New Zealand stands as a quiet rebellion. His harvest is more than just a crop; it is a distillation of the ancient forest itself, gathered by hand, and deeply connected to the wild earth from which it grew.

Precautions & Contraindications

While Mānuka leaves are generally safe, certain precautions should be taken.

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding women should consult a healthcare professional before use.
  • Individuals with allergies to Mānuka or related plants should avoid using.
  • Please consult a healthcare professional if you are taking medications.

How to Brew

  1. Boil 220 mL water to 100 degrees Celsius.

  2. Put a tea bag into a cup.

  3. Pour hot water into the cup.

  4. Wait for 3 minutes.

*Adding milk to the beverage is not recommended.
