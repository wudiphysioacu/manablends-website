Therapeutic Benefits of Sweet Tea

Sweet tea (Lithocarpus polystachyus Rehd.) has been consumed as herbal tea to prevent and manage diabetes for a long time. Recent studies indicate that sweet tea is rich in a variety of bioactive compounds, especially a class of nonclassical flavonoids, dihydrochalcones. In order to provide a better understanding of sweet tea and its main dihydrochalcones on human health, this review mainly summarizes related literature in the recent ten years, with the potential molecular mechanisms emphatically discussed. Phlorizin, phloretin, and trilobatin, three natural sweeteners, are the main dihydrochalcones in sweet tea. In addition, sweet tea and its dihydrochalcones exhibit plenty of health benefits, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, cardioprotective, hepatoprotective, antidiabetic, and anticancer effects, which are associated with the regulation of different molecular targets and signaling pathways. Therefore, sweet tea, as a rare natural source of dihydrochalcones, can be processed and developed into nutraceuticals or functional foods, with the potential application in the prevention and management of certain chronic diseases.

Potential Therapeutic Benefits of Rosemary

Emotion & Nerve System

  • Cognitive Enhancement

    Rosemary contains compounds like carnosic acid, which may improve memory, focus, and protect brain cells from oxidative stress.

  • Mood and Stress Relief

    Aromatherapy with rosemary oil is linked to reduced stress and fatigue. It also enhances mental clarity.

Immune System

  • Antioxidant Properties

    Rosemary is rich in antioxidants, such as rosmarinic acid (RA). These antioxidants combat free radicals, reducing oxidative damage linked to aging and chronic diseases.

  • Anti-Inflammatory Effects

    Rosemary may alleviate inflammation. This could potentially aid conditions like arthritis or muscle pain.

  • Immune Boost

    Rosemary has antimicrobial properties. These properties may help fight bacterial infections and support immune function.

Organ System

  • Digestive Support

    Rosemary has been traditionally used to soothe indigestion, bloating, and cramps. It stimulates bile production, which aids in digestion.

  • Hair and Scalp Health

    Rosemary oil is popular for promoting hair growth. It can also reduce dandruff and improve scalp circulation.

The Harvest

The Scent of the Earth: A Labor of Love on a New Zealand Rosemary Farm

As the early morning mist rolls off the emerald hills of New Zealand, the air grows crisp and deeply fragrant. Here, tucked away on a boutique smallholding, the day begins not with the roar of heavy machinery, but with the quiet, deliberate footsteps of a local farmer. This is the home of an artisanal rosemary harvest—a process entirely dependent on the careful work of human hands.

The Harvest: Snip by Snip

Long before the sun reaches its peak, the farmer is out in the field. Manual harvesting is a deliberate choice, born from a deep respect for the plant. Armed with sharp secateurs, the farmer moves down the rows, carefully selecting the most robust branches. With every cut, the air fills with the sharp, pine-like scent of fresh rosemary. The essential oils are at their most potent in the cool morning air, and by hand-cutting the stems, the farmer ensures the plant is not bruised or damaged, preserving its vitality for the next season. By the end of the morning, the farmer's hands are sticky with fragrant resin—a testament to the herb's rich quality.

The Sorting: A Meticulous Eye

Once baskets are overflowing with green sprigs, the harvest is carried into the packing shed for the sorting process. This requires patience and a sharp eye. The farmer inspects each stem individually, gently running their fingers over the needle-like leaves. Any yellowing leaves, overly woody stalks, or tiny hitchhiking insects are meticulously discarded. Only the most pristine, vibrant sprigs make the cut. It is a slow, meditative process that guarantees only the highest grade of rosemary moves forward.

The Wash: Pure New Zealand Water

The selected rosemary is then bathed in cool, clear water. The washing process is gentle but thorough. Submerged in large basins, the sprigs are lightly agitated to rinse away the dust and soil blown in by the coastal winds. The cold water shocks the leaves, making them appear even brighter and more vibrant. As the dirt washes away, the pure, unadulterated essence of the herb is left behind, clean and glistening.

The Drying: Patience and Air

The final step is perhaps the most crucial: drying. The washed rosemary is gently shaken and laid out in single layers on custom-built wooden drying racks. Unlike commercial operations that use harsh kiln dryers, this small farmer relies on the natural environment. Placed in a well-ventilated barn out of direct, scorching sunlight, the rosemary is left to air-dry slowly. The gentle New Zealand breeze does the work, evaporating the moisture over several days while locking in the volatile oils, the rich green color, and the robust flavor.

A Connection to the Land

When the drying is finally complete, the rosemary crackles slightly between the fingers, its aroma concentrated and powerful. What began as a dewy bush in a New Zealand paddock has been transformed through careful, manual labor into a premium culinary herb. It is a slow, hands-on journey from soil to spice, reflecting the true spirit of small-scale farming: quality, patience, and a profound connection to the land.

Precautions & Contraindications

While Rosemary is generally safe, certain precautions should be taken.

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding women should consult a healthcare professional before use.
  • Individuals with allergies to Rosemary or related plants should avoid using.
  • Please consult a healthcare professional if you are taking medications.

How to Brew

  1. Boil 220 mL water to 100 degrees Celsius.

  2. Put a tea bag into a cup.

  3. Pour hot water into the cup.

  4. Wait for 3 minutes.
